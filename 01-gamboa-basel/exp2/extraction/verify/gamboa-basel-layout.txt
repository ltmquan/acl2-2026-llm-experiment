      Leonhard Euler showed in 1735 that the sum of the squares of the reciprocals of the positive integers
                   2
      is exactly π6 . The problem was originally posed in 1650, but it eluded even the greatest mathematical
      minds of the time, including the Bernoullis, Leibniz, Stirling, and de Moivre. When a young Euler
      solved this problem, his reputation as a top mathematician was ensured. In this paper, we present a
      proof in ACL2(r) of this remarkable result. Rather than formalizing Euler’s original proof, we use a
      more modern argument that is more suitable to the logic of ACL2(r). This proof is an entirely alge-
      braic version of the “Lighthouse Proof” which uses a geometric argument to motivate the appearance
      of π in the final formula.


1    Introduction
                             1
The harmonic series ∑∞   i=1 i is well known to be divergent, but what if the terms are squared? That is,
            1
does ∑∞ i=1 i2 converge, and if so, what does it converge to?
    The convergence of this series is not difficult to establish. The series has only positive terms, so it is
increasing. Moreover, it is not too difficult to find an upper bound for it. In Sec. 3, we show that 2 is a
suitable upper bound. Those two facts are enough to guarantee convergence.
    But the actual limit is much more difficult to find. Euler was the first mathematician to find this limit,
and he did so in 1734, nearly 100 years after the problem was posed. At the time, Euler was living in the
city of Basel, Switzerland, as did the Bernoullis, who had tried and failed to find this limit. Hence the
problem of finding this limit became known as the “Basel Problem.”
                                                      2
    What Euler found is that the sum converges to π6 , which is remarkable since the sum itself is a simple
recursive function entirely in the language of ACL2, but the right-hand side introduces the irrational
number π, which (being irrational) is entirely outside the language of ACL2. Hence, ACL2(r) is required
even to state the theorem.
    The ACL2(r) variant of ACL2 introduces the irrational numbers into ACL2. It is based on non-
standard analysis, which provides a rigorous framework for reasoning about notions such as “nearly
zero,” “infinitely large,” and “almost equal” (i-small, i-large, and i-close in ACL2(r)). Sec. 2
provides a brief overview of the concepts from non-standard analysis that are relevant to this paper.
Note that in the sequel we will use the terms ACL2 and ACL2(r) interchangeably, but that this entire
formalization must be carried out in ACL2(r).

2    Preliminaries
Non-standard analysis is a rigorous framework for reasoning about analysis, using the intuitive notions of
“infinitely small” and “infinitely large” numbers that Newton and Leibniz used in their original develop-
ment of calculus. This formalization of analysis is better suited to ACL2 than Cauchy and Weierstrass’s
ε-δ approach, because it replaces quantifier reasoning (e.g., ∀ε > 0, ∃δ > 0 such that . . . ), with simpler
arguments that rely only on algebra and induction. There are a number of excellent introductions to
non-standard analysis, including [6, 5]. This brief section provides a high-level overview of the concepts
that are most relevant to this paper.
     The non-standard real line looks just like the normal real line, but some real numbers are called
“standard.” All the real numbers we’ve ever encountered in “normal” mathematics, e.g., 0, 1, π, e, etc.,
are standard. But there are also non-standard numbers, some of which are larger than all the standard
positive numbers, and some of which are positive, yet smaller than all the standard positive numbers.
These small numbers are sometimes called “infinitesimals” and are suggestive of the ε in the ε-δ defini-
tion of limits. All numbers that are limited, that is bounded by some standard number, can be written as
x = ∗ x + ε, where ∗ x is a standard number called the (unique) standard part of x, and ε is an infinitesimal.
The standard part of a standard number is the number itself, and the standard part function obeys nice
algebraic properties, such as ∗ (x + y) = ∗ x + ∗ y and x ≤ y ⇒ ∗ x ≤ ∗ y. However, ∗ (x · y) = ∗ x · ∗ y only holds
if x and y are limited.
     A sequence a1 , a2 , a3 , . . . of real numbers converges to a limit L if ∗ an = L for all infinitely large n.
Equivalently, the sequence converges if ai is limited and ai ≈ a j for all infinitely large i and j. Once
the basic concept of limit is established, the rest of calculus follows. I.e., the property of continuity,
derivatives, and integrals are all defined in terms of limits, just as in the ε-δ approach.

As we will see in Sec. 6, this is enough to show the final result. Unfortunately, the argument above is
deeply flawed. The problem is twofold. First, we are being careless with the notion of “nearly zero.”
For another, as n grows, each individual term may be approaching a limit, but the number of terms in the
sum is also growing, so the limit of the sum is not necessarily equal to the sum of the limits. It is part of
a mathematician’s education to recognize when arguments of this sort are valid and when they are not.
     That is also part of the rigor that is built into non-standard analysis, as it makes the notions such as
“nearly zero” and “infinite” very formal and precise. In ACL2(r), this rigor is reflected in a number of
restrictions on the use of these “non-classical” notions. One such restriction is that induction cannot be
used to prove arbitrary theorems about all natural numbers. If the theorem you wish to prove mentions
(directly or indirectly) any of these non-classical notions, then induction can only be used to prove the
theorem for standard natural numbers, which is to say for the “finite” natural numbers. This is a serious
restriction, and it applies in this case because the theorem we are trying to prove is that the sum in the
                                                                   2
left-hand side of the equation is “almost equal” to the term π8 . The restriction is sufficient to keep us
from falling into the trap laid out by the seductive argument above.

about a property that uses non-classical notions, we proceed by proving some lemmas that do not use
non-classical notions, and instead result in some terms that depend on n. Once that is done, we can see
how the terms simplify for large values of n.

A trivial induction shows that the sum of the even terms is just a quarter of the sum of the entire series.

That completes the proof of the Basel problem in ACL2(r), although we prefer to state it slightly differ-

which leaves the Basel series in its original form, and is closer in spirit to the idea of limits. Thus, we
restate the final theorem as follows:
( defthmd b a s e l - s e r i e s - c l o s e d - f o r m - c l o s e
   ( implies ( and ( posp n )
                        ( i-large n ))
              ( i-close ( basel-series 1 n )
                                (/ ( expt ( acl2-pi ) 2) 6)))
   : hints ...)


7    Conclusion
In this paper, we presented a formalization of the Basel problem in ACL2(r). Euler’s original proof
worked by factoring the sine function into an infinite product, and formalizing this idea directly would
have presented some formidable challenges in ACL2(r). For one thing, ACL2 does not have infinite sets,
so any discussion of an infinite product is problematic. For another, the justification of the factorization
itself requires the Weierstrass factorization theorem from complex analysis, which is a deep result not yet
formalized in ACL2(r). So we followed a different path, which is an algebraic treatment of a geometric
proof of the Basel problem.
     A key part of the proof is that the sum of the even terms in the Basel series is exactly a quarter of the
sum of the entire series. This idea can be generalized to show that the sum of the Basel series can also
be expressed as a product over the primes, showing the deep connections between what we now call the
Riemann zeta function and prime numbers. The Euler product formula states that
